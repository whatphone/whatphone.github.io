How Big Can You Print a Phone Photo?

Pixels, PPI and aspect ratio explained, with print sizes for 12, 48, 50, 108 and 200 megapixel phone cameras.

October 29, 2026
5 minute read
WhatPhone Editorial

Your phone camera takes photos that look sharp on a screen, but a wall print is a different test. Print size depends on pixels, not on how good the picture looks on a display. This guide explains how to work out the largest sharp print from a phone photo, what PPI to aim for and how to avoid the common mistakes that lead to soft prints.

For a quick answer, use our photo print size calculator: enter your pixels or pick a camera preset and see the largest print and the quality for every common paper size.

Step 1: Find Your Photo's Pixel Size

Open the photo in your gallery and look at its Info or Details view. You will see dimensions such as 4032 × 3024. Multiply them and divide by one million to get megapixels, so 4032 × 3024 is about 12.2 MP.

Many phones with 48, 50, 108 or 200 megapixel sensors save 12 MP photos by default, combining pixels for better light. You only get the full resolution if you switch to the camera's high-resolution mode, so check the details of the file you plan to print rather than the number on the box.

Step 2: Divide Pixels by PPI

PPI means pixels per inch. To find the print size, divide each pixel dimension by the PPI you want: inches = pixels ÷ PPI. At 300 PPI, a 4032 × 3024 photo prints at 13.4 × 10.1 inches. At 200 PPI the same photo reaches about 20 × 15 inches.

Step 3: Pick the Right PPI for the Viewing Distance

1

300 PPI for prints held in the hand

Photo books, 4×6 and 5×7 prints and anything people look at closely look best at 300 PPI or more.

2

200 PPI for framed wall prints

Most framed pictures are seen from an arm's length or further, and 200 PPI looks sharp at that distance.

3

150 PPI or lower for posters

Large posters and canvases are viewed from several feet away, so 150 PPI is acceptable and even lower can work for billboards.

These numbers are guidelines. A well-exposed, sharp photo prints better at 150 PPI than a blurry one at 300.

Step 4: Watch the Aspect Ratio and Cropping

Most phone photos have a 4:3 shape. Common print sizes have different shapes: 4×6 is 3:2, 8×10 is 5:4, and A4 is about 1:1.41. To fill the paper without white borders, the photo is cropped, which removes pixels and lowers the PPI of what remains. The calculator uses the filled, cropped size and marks sizes that crop, so you know when to leave extra space around your subject before you shoot.

Step 5: Prepare the File and Order the Print

1

Send the original, not a chat copy

Apps like WhatsApp and Messenger shrink and compress photos. Send the original file, or use the option to send as a document, so the lab gets all the pixels.

2

Use JPEG if the lab asks for it

If a lab does not accept HEIC, export a JPEG. On iPhone you can switch Settings → Camera → Formats → Most Compatible.

3

Check the lab's recommended size

Most print labs list the minimum pixels for each size. Compare it with your photo's dimensions.

4

Do not upscale a small photo

Enlarging a low-resolution photo adds no real detail. If a size is too big, pick a smaller print or step back to a larger source file.

5

Edit lightly and check the screen

Print colors are usually a little darker than a bright screen. Slightly raise the brightness or order a small test print first.

✅
Pro tip: Hold the photo at 100% zoom on your phone. If it looks soft there, it will look soft in a large print as well.
